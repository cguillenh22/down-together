**Quick answer.** "Special education" is a service, not a place — it's the supports and accommodations your child receives. "Inclusion" is where that service happens: in the regular classroom, alongside peers, instead of in a separate room. A child can receive high-quality special education while being fully included.

**Why it matters.** It's easy to assume "special education" automatically means "separate classroom" — but that idea confuses the service with the place it's delivered in, and that confusion ends up narrowing the options the school offers you.

**What the source says.** NDSS's Inclusive Education Guidelines distinguish three models that often get blended in conversation: full segregation (separate classroom all day), mainstreaming (occasional visits to the regular classroom for specific, usually non-academic subjects), and inclusion (participation in the regular classroom for most or all of the day, with supports like a co-teacher, adapted materials, or team teaching). The evidence cited in those guidelines is consistent: children with Down syndrome show better academic and social outcomes in inclusive models than in segregated ones, even accounting for the level of support they need.

*United States.* NDSS frames this model (segregation / mainstreaming / inclusion) as part of how IDEA's "least restrictive environment" principle plays out in practice.

*Spain.* The system is formally dual: there are "centros ordinarios" (mainstream schools) and "centros específicos" (special schools), and Down21/Down España considers placing a child in a special school to be, in their words, a form of discrimination incompatible with the UN Convention — a firmer position than NDSS takes.

*United Kingdom.* The formal choice is between "mainstream school" and "special school," and supports arrive through an EHC (Education, Health and Care) plan rather than a separate "service" category from the placement itself (DSA).

Same underlying idea, different institutional language and a different level of legal firmness — worth stating explicitly rather than treating any one country's framework as universal.

**What can you do now?** When you talk with the school, explicitly separate the two questions: "what supports does my child need?" (special education) and "where will they receive them?" (inclusion). These are separate decisions, and treating them as one is where the most options get lost.
